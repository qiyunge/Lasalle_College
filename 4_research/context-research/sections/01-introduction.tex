
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern large language models (LLMs) acquire knowledge and capabilities through
pre-training and post-training. During inference, their model parameters are
typically fixed, while the input context can vary substantially across tasks
and interactions.

Consequently, model performance depends not only on the capabilities learned
during training, but also on whether relevant information in the provided
context can be effectively utilized.

A simplified view of inference is

\[
    y_t = f_{\theta}(C_t),
\]

where $\theta$ represents the fixed model parameters, $C_t$ represents the
context provided at inference time, and $y_t$ represents the model output.

This distinction motivates a fundamental question: even when relevant
information is available within the model's context window, can the model
reliably use it?

The present study approaches this question through controlled experiments on
information position, context length, and potentially information interference.

\subsection{From Model Capabilities to Effective Behavior}

The knowledge and capabilities acquired during training are represented through
the model's learned parameters. However, possessing a capability does not
guarantee that it will be expressed consistently under every inference
condition.

In particular, the accessibility and organization of information within the
context may influence the model's ability to produce a correct response.

Conceptually,

\[
    \text{Learned Capability}
    +
    \text{Appropriate Context}
    \rightarrow
    \text{Effective Behavior}.
\]

This relationship is a conceptual motivation rather than a deterministic
guarantee of performance.

It highlights the distinction between the information available to a model and
the information that the model can effectively utilize.

Therefore, evaluating context utilization requires more than measuring the
maximum number of tokens a model can accept.

\subsection{Context Engineering and Information Utilization}

Context engineering concerns the selection, organization, and presentation of
information supplied to a model during inference.

A context may contain instructions, conversation history, retrieved evidence,
tool outputs, and other task-relevant information:

\[
    C_t =
    \{
        \text{instructions},
        \text{history},
        \text{evidence},
        \text{tools},
        \text{state}
    \}.
\]

While context engineering addresses how information is provided, context
utilization concerns how effectively the model uses that information to perform
a task.

These are related but distinct problems. A system may successfully include the
necessary evidence in its context without obtaining the correct answer.

For example, relevant evidence may appear near the beginning, middle, or end of
a long input. Its relative position may influence retrieval accuracy even when
the evidence itself remains unchanged.

Similarly, increasing the total context length may introduce additional
irrelevant information and alter the model's ability to locate and use the
target evidence.

This motivates studying context utilization as an empirical property of model
behavior rather than assuming that all information within the context window is
equally accessible.

\subsection{Motivation from Agentic Systems}

The importance of effective context utilization becomes particularly apparent
in agentic systems, where models interact with external tools and accumulate
information over multiple steps.

A simplified agent loop can be represented as
\[
    \text{Observe}
    \rightarrow
    \text{Reason}
    \rightarrow
    \text{Act}
    \rightarrow
    \text{Observe}.
\]

During long-running execution, conversation history, retrieved documents,
source code, tool outputs, and previous decisions may accumulate beyond the
available context budget.

Let $H_t$ represent the information available to the system and $C_t$ the
working context selected for a particular inference. In many long-running
settings,

\[
    |H_t| > |C_t|.
\]

A context-construction policy $\pi$ may therefore select information for the
model:

\[
    H_t
    \xrightarrow{\pi}
    C_t
    \xrightarrow{f_{\theta}}
    y_t.
\]

However, selecting relevant information is only part of the problem. The model
must also be able to utilize the selected information effectively.

Understanding how information position and context length influence model
performance may therefore inform the design of future context-selection and
context-management strategies.

The present study does not attempt to implement or optimize such a strategy.
Agentic systems instead provide a practical motivation for investigating the
underlying behavior of long-context language models.

\subsection{Research Objective and Scope}

The broader objective of this research is to investigate how three factors
influence effective context utilization:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The position of relevant evidence within the context.
    \item The total length of the context.
    \item Interference from semantically similar distractors.
\end{itemize}

The primary experimental investigation focuses on the relationship between
evidence position and context length, while maintaining a controlled baseline
distractor condition.

Specifically, the study examines whether positional performance differences
persist in modern LLMs and whether their magnitude changes as context length
increases.

A potential extension will investigate whether semantically similar but
irrelevant information further affects this relationship, depending on
available time and computational resources.

The study uses fixed model parameters and controlled retrieval tasks to examine
these effects. It does not propose a new language model or a complete adaptive
context-management system.

The central motivation can be summarized by two distinctions:

\[
    \boxed{
        \text{More Context}
        \not\equiv
        \text{Better Context}
    }
\]

and

\[
    \boxed{
        \text{Available Information}
        \not\equiv
        \text{Effectively Utilized Information}
    }.
\]

By characterizing these relationships, the research aims to provide empirical
evidence that may inform future context engineering and agentic system design.
